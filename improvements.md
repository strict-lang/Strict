1. **LLM inference on the GPU** (asked 2026-10-15, RTX 4090 24 GB, Threadripper 3990X, 128 GB): reach and pass llama.cpp with the same GGUF weights, prompts and greedy sampling, first Qwen3-8B Q4_K_M at batch 1, then batch 8 x 4K. Measured with `NeuralNetworks/Qwen3Benchmark.strict <gguf> 4 <tokens>` (warmup generation, prefill and decode per token apart, median), llama.cpp b11658 CUDA (`llama-bench -p 512 -n 128`) on the same machine: Qwen3-8B Q4_K_M decode 8.2-8.8 ms (113-122 tokens/s, 180 ms on the CPU, 205 ms before the f32 sums) against 7.3 ms (136.5 tokens/s), prefill 2.5-4.4 ms per token (one step per prompt token, llama.cpp batches 512 at 9413 tokens/s), loaded in 6-7 s (was 17-19 s); Qwen3 0.6B Q4_K_M 2.4-3.1 ms (noisy, 320-415 tokens/s, 28-33 ms on the CPU) against 2.2 ms (459 tokens/s). Done so far: loops over packed numbers calculate in 32 bit floats on both backends (`Compiler/MlirSingle`, the f32 twin of every kernel), their results stay on the GPU (README "Native model"), launches do not wait and are captured into CUDA graphs of 48 that run again with updated arguments (`STRICT_GRAPHS=0` launches directly, a cache of 256 graphs probed 8 deep by a print of their kernels and copies), rows of 4 and 6 bits are the Q4_K and Q6_K blocks of ggml (`strict.h`: 4.5 and 6.56 bits per weight instead of 6 and 10, the loader copies instead of repacking, the CPU decodes 32 numbers per vector operation), 8/16/32 bit rows too, one width per row, the dot product decodes 8 (Q4_K) or 16 (Q6_K) numbers per lane without int to float conversions (they run on the slow FP64 pipe of consumer GPUs), small host lists move to the GPU on their second use (a hashed cache of 4096 entries), the whole Qwen3 step runs on the GPU (`Qwen3Block` with flat queries, keys and values, per head norms, rotation and softmax as loops), the argmax reads the block maxima and one block of 128 logits, GPU results are collected when half of the free device memory was taken since the last collection (was every 1 GB: a 125 ms collection every 130 tokens). Per 8B token now: 7.2 ms of kernels (`Layer.Sums` 5.5 ms at about 730 GB/s of 4.0 GB, the 151936 logits 0.55 ms at 920 GB/s), 759 launches at about 2 us of host time each, the GPU idles 1-1.5 ms: 0.4 ms of host work before the first kernel of a step (the first `Normalized` reads the embedding row on the CPU, 0.2 ms, and the two downloads of the argmax), 45-170 us per graph launch, a new 64 MB device chunk every 6 steps. Next, in order: the start of a step (small loops reading packed host lists on the GPU, one download for the argmax); a generational collector (a full collection marks the 7.6 GB of host weights in 125 ms, GPU results are only recycled by collections, a young generation would free each step's 11 MB right away); fewer launches (fusing a loop into the next one when it only feeds it element by element, `Scales` into `Normalized`, the softmax into `Merged`); quantized activations with dp4a (the mmvq kernels of llama.cpp); FlashAttention style prefill (all prompt tokens in one step, the matrix products then read every weight once per prompt); attention that reads the cache coalesced at long contexts (`Merged` and `Scores` read one position per lane); batched decoding (shared weights, per sequence caches, positions and sampling, continuous batching, paged and FP8 caches, prefix caching, CPU offloading of inactive caches) with batch 1/2/4/8/16 benchmarks; logit comparisons with transformers for the 8B; later pruning and speculative decoding experiments (start with GPT-2), Qwen3-30B-A3B against Qwen3-8B. Each optimization stays switchable (`STRICT_GPU=0` for the CPU, `STRICT_GRAPHS=0`, a runtime change is one function) and gets measured before the next.
2. **Neural networks** (the use case): `NeuralNetworks/` has a multilayer perceptron written in Strict (see its README): `Layer` (fully connected, sigmoid, one weight row per neuron), `Network` (backpropagation over any number of layers, softmax with cross entropy for several outputs, momentum 0.5, weights start in plus minus 1 / square root of the inputs), `Csv` and `Dataset` (features scaled to 0..1, one-hot classes, every fifth sample held out, a new random order every epoch). Natively XOR (2-3-1) trains in 11 ms, Iris (4-8-3) in 25 ms with 97% test accuracy, Diabetes (19-10-2 on a quarter of the UCI Diabetic Retinopathy Debrecen data, 288 rows) in 80 ms with 60-64%. The MNIST digits moved into their own repository (github.com/strict-lang/Mnist, `../Mnist`): 256-32-4 neurons reach 100% on 32 held out 16x16 images in 0.49 s natively (58 s on the Strict VM), 784-64-6 neurons 68-82% on 28x28 images in 2.8 s. Every step still builds new lists for the activations, deltas and weights, and a method call with loop-invariant arguments runs on every iteration natively (the compiler could hoist it). A `for` over a list or a count whose body only loads variables, calculates numbers, reads lists at `index` and collects one value per element runs twice compiled (`Compiler/MlirKernel`, `MlirFastLoop`): when every list read at `index` is at least as long as the loop, a copy with an integer counter, direct loads and direct stores into the presized result runs (LLVM vectorizes it), otherwise the checked loop, where a too short list stops the method with the index Error. Measured 2026-10-14: an element-wise update of 784 numbers 3.15 to 0.75 ns per element, a dot product 1.12 to 0.35 ns, MNIST 256-32-4 0.48 to 0.12 s, 784-64-6 2.74 to 0.86 s, Iris 27 to 20 ms. Now about 38% of the 784-64-6 training is allocating and collecting (`strict_gc_allocate`, `next_span`, `memset`, mark): every step builds new weight rows, the previous weights are kept for the momentum and the ones before become garbage. Native code is built with `-march=native` and the add of a sum in such a loop (`sum = sum + ...`) may be reordered (README), so LLVM vectorizes sums (AVX2 with 4 or 16 partial sums). Loops with calls (`activation.Output(...)`, `Row(...)` per row) still take the checked path. On the Strict VM every number is a `VmValue` with four members, each arithmetic result allocates one. Next: bigger networks to measure parallel `for` on all CPU cores, SIMD, GPU and CUDA through MLIR, compared with the C# version. Data over 1 MB is downloaded when needed instead of being checked in. The old C# code in `C:\code\AiInvest\AiInvest.NeuralNetworks` is only a guide, it has bugs (MatrixNetwork backprop drops the hidden gradient, the Tanh, Swish and Logistic derivatives, CrossEntropy ignores the target). Long term a more abstract language uses Strict like DNA: long chains of simple instructions that are easy to copy, change, merge and test, which the higher level rewrites, tests and evolves to reach its goals.
